Art Assets

Everything the game draws today is drawn in code (js/art.js and the minigame files). This page lists every picture the game would need to swap that for ready-made art, with the size and rules for each, so the list can be handed to an artist or an image generator. The lists below are built from the game data, so they stay current as the island grows.

Decisions so far: the tile size is not settled yet, so every size is given at 16 px to the tile (as now) and at 32 px (twice the detail); the main cast (35 people) get a sprite sheet each, and the other 95 islanders are built from layered parts; night stays the game's own lighting for now; and the list covers everything, films and minigames included.

How many: about 32 outdoor ground maps (built from tile sets), 116 building designs, 139 rooms, 201 kinds of furniture, 78 kinds of prop, 35 character sheets, the layered parts for the rest, 130 portraits, 107 inventory icons, 12 tattoo designs, and the screens, films and games.

Rules for every asset

  • Look: top-down pixel art, seen from above at a slight angle (the fronts of buildings, furniture and people show; roofs and tops are seen from above), in the manner of 1990s handheld role-playing games. Early 1980s, Cold War, a snowy island north of Finland. Each town has its own look (docs/town-aesthetics.md): Eastbrook timber and brick, Frostholm neon, Saltwick a run-down fishing town, Hope Springs wealthy villas, Kingsgrad a light steampunk town of copper roofs and chimney stacks.
  • Grid: everything sits on the tile grid: 16×16 px tiles now, or 32×32 if the art is made at double size. The screen shows 15×10 tiles (240×160 px, or 480×320). Sizes on this page are in tiles, then pixels at 16 and (in brackets) at 32.
  • Pixels: hard-edged pixels at exactly the stated size: no blur, no anti-aliasing, no soft shadows, no gradients wider than a few colours. One pixel of the art is one pixel of the grid; the game scales it up whole.
  • Light: from the upper left, so the left and top edges are lighter and the right and bottom darker. Don't bake in a cast shadow on the ground: the game draws people's shadows itself.
  • Colour: one shared palette for everything (about 48 to 64 colours), cold and muted: snow whites and blue-greys, dark pine greens, weathered wood, red and ochre accents; brighter neon only in Frostholm.
  • Files: PNG, with a transparent background for anything that isn't a full-screen picture. One file per asset, named by the id in the tables below (assets/buildings/diner.png, assets/items/islandmap.png); animations as a strip of frames left to right, all the same size.
  • Snow: most of the island is under snow: roofs carry it, ledges have a white line, things stand in it. A few places have no snow (see Outdoor places): their buildings and trees need a no-snow version.
  • Text: signs and labels are part of the art (the table gives each sign's words); keep text to capital letters a few pixels high, as now.
  • Height: things taller than their footprint (buildings, lamp posts, wardrobes) rise above it: draw the footprint at the bottom of the image and let the rest go up. Each table says how far.

Ground tiles

The ground is built from tiles, not painted whole. Each terrain needs a tile set: a plain tile or three for variety, and the edges where it meets the ground around it (a 16-piece set: the four sides, four outer corners, four inner corners and the middle; or a 47-piece set for rounder joins). Animated ones are a strip of 4 frames.

Everyone else: layered parts (95 people)

The other islanders are put together in the game from parts drawn on the same 16×20 sheet layout (every part a full 48×80 sheet, transparent where it has nothing, so they stack exactly). Draw the parts in light grey where they take the person's own colour, so the game can tint them (clothes, hair), and in their real colours where they don't (skin, faces, buttons). The player's wardrobe and the character creator at Langley use the same parts, so they serve both.

  • Bodies: a man and a woman, in 4 skin tones (8 sheets), with faces; legs in trousers, and a woman's legs in dark tights under a skirt.
  • Hair: short, side parting, curly, buzz cut, bald (a fringe at the sides), long, bob, ponytail, bun (9, tinted).
  • Facial hair: full beard, moustache, stubble (3, tinted).
  • Tops: by style: parka, jumper, flannel shirt, suit, trench coat, and a plain work top (tinted); an apron over it, for cooks, bakers and bar staff.
  • Legs: jeans, trousers, corduroys, dungarees, a skirt (tinted).
  • Shoes: boots, snow boots, sneakers, shoes, cowboy boots (tinted).
  • Hats: knitted beanie, flat cap, ushanka, beret, fedora, Stetson, sou'wester, a peaked uniform cap (soldiers and police) and a jester's cap with bells in two colours (tinted).

Portraits

A head-and-shoulders picture of each person, shown beside what they say in a conversation, in the middle of the screen: 28×28 px (or 56×56) now, though a portrait can be larger than the world art (64×64 or 96×96 reads better) as long as every one is the same size. Facing slightly to the right, on a plain background the game fills in, in the same palette.

The main cast (35) need one each, made to match their sheet. For everyone else either a portrait each (95 more), or portrait parts layered like their sprites (faces in 4 skin tones for men and women, the 9 hair styles, 3 facial hairs, the hats, a collar per top style): fewer pictures, less character. The player's portrait is built from the parts.

Night

For now, night stays the game's own lighting: it darkens the day art, cuts pools of light around lamps, fires and windows, and makes neon glow and flicker. So the day art is enough, plus two small extras that let the lighting work:

  • Lit windows: for each building, a second picture the same size with only its window panes in a warm yellow (everything else transparent), laid over the building at night.
  • Signs that glow: neon signs as their own layer (letters only), so the game can light them, flicker them and leave a dead letter dark (the D on Aili's Diner).
  • Lights: nothing: lamp posts, braziers and fires already mark where the light comes from.

An option for later: separate painted night art: a night version of every ground tile set, building and outdoor prop (Frostholm and its harbour purple neon, everywhere else moonlit blue). It looks richer but nearly doubles the outdoor list, and the game's lighting would still add the lamps on top.
